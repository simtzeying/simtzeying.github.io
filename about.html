My story

I came to computing education research not through a grand plan, but through something I saw in my own classroom that I simply could not unsee.

Early in my career as a university programming lecturer, I noticed that my first-year students' marks did not form the bell curve I expected. They formed a U-shape. Students scoring As had one thing in common: they had been exposed to programming before university. Students with Ds and Fs had another thing in common: they had not. The class was not dividing along lines of intelligence or effort. It was dividing along lines of access.

"The difference was not ability. It was access. And that gap had been built into the system long before any of my students walked into my classroom."

When I looked into why, the picture became clear. Computer Science is an elective subject at SPM level — Malaysia's national secondary school examination — and it is largely offered by schools that already have computer laboratories, qualified teachers, and the infrastructure to support it. Better-equipped and private schools tend to have these resources. Under-resourced national schools often do not. The result is that students from lower-income communities arrive at university already behind, through no fault of their own.

That observation became a question I have spent my career trying to answer: how do we make quality computing education genuinely available to every student, regardless of where they grow up, which school they attend, or what resources their family can afford?

My research uses low-cost, accessible technology — particularly the BBC micro:bit — to bring programming education into schools that cannot afford dedicated computer laboratories. Through the BEST Programme, we reached over 200 lower secondary students across three Malaysian schools. Through collaborations with universities in the United Kingdom, El Salvador, and Finland, we are testing and scaling this approach internationally. The work is empirically grounded, teacher-informed, and aligned with SDG 4 (Quality Education) and SDG 10 (Reducing Inequalities).

I am an educator by heart and a technologist in practice. I believe technology can bring about positive transformation when it is designed with the right values — and that education is one of the most powerful engines of social mobility we have. My goal is to make sure that engine is available to everyone, not just those born close to it.
