Teaching

Twenty-plus years of teaching computing at university level — from introductory programming to advanced software engineering. I believe the best teaching happens when students are challenged to think, not just to follow instructions.

Teaching philosophy

My U-curve observation — that first-year programming students' marks split sharply along lines of prior access rather than ability — is not just a research finding. It is a challenge I try to address in every class I teach. The students who arrive without computing backgrounds are not less capable. They need a different starting point, more scaffolding in the early stages, and explicit instruction in the thinking skills that more experienced students have already absorbed.

I design my courses to be active and participatory. Programming cannot be learned by watching — it requires doing, making mistakes, debugging, and trying again. I integrate problem-based activities, peer collaboration, and low-stakes formative assessment so that students can build confidence alongside competence. I am particularly attentive to helping first-generation computing students find their footing.

"A good teacher does not just transfer knowledge. A good teacher changes what a student believes they are capable of."

⬡

Learning by doing

Programming is a craft. I build in-class activities, labs, and projects that require students to construct solutions — not just recognise them. Struggle is part of the process.

⬡

Thinking before coding

Computational thinking comes before syntax. I spend time explicitly teaching decomposition, abstraction, and algorithmic reasoning — especially for students without prior computing exposure.

⬡

Assessment for learning

My assessments are designed to reveal understanding, not just recall. I use iterative assignments and reflective tasks that reward the ability to explain, adapt, and improve.
