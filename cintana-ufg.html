Cintana–UFG Collaboration

An international research partnership between Sunway University (Malaysia) and Universidad Francisco Gavidia (El Salvador), funded by Cintana Education in collaboration with Arizona State University — testing and scaling unplugged computational thinking and micro:bit-based programming activities across two developing countries.

Collaboration background

The unplugged computational thinking methodology developed in Malaysia through the FRGS grant raised a critical question: does it work only in a Malaysian context, or is it genuinely scalable across different countries, cultures, and resource environments?

The Cintana–UFG collaboration was formed to answer that question. Through Cintana Education's international grant programme — which connects Arizona State University's global education network with partner institutions — Sunway University and Universidad Francisco Gavidia (UFG) in El Salvador undertook two related but distinct studies, each testing how low-resource computing education approaches perform in a different developing country context.

"If an approach to computing education only works in one country, it is not a solution — it is a local fix. The real test is whether it can travel."

El Salvador and Malaysia share a common challenge: schools in under-resourced communities often lack the computer laboratories and specialist teachers needed to deliver conventional programming education. The collaboration tested whether the same approach — carefully designed activities that require no devices, or only affordable hardware — could produce meaningful learning outcomes in both contexts.

Project 1 · Completed Cintana International Grant  ·  USD 2,000
2024 – 2025

Exploration of the impact of unplugged computational thinking activities on computing interest and performance

What we did

This study replicated and extended the Malaysian unplugged CT activities in an El Salvador context, working with UFG students and school-level participants. The same core activity design principles were applied — physical, hands-on, non-device exercises that teach computational thinking through problem-solving, sequencing, and collaboration.

The study measured two outcomes: computing interest (whether students became more interested in programming and computer science after the activities) and computing performance (whether their ability to solve programming-style problems improved). Crucially, results from El Salvador were compared directly with parallel findings from Malaysia to assess cross-cultural generalisability.

Outcomes & evidence

Approach validated across two countries

The unplugged CT activities produced measurable improvements in computing interest and performance in both Malaysia and El Salvador — confirming the methodology generalises across different cultural and resource contexts.

Scalability case established

The comparative study provides evidence that this approach to inclusive STEM education is not context-dependent — it can be adopted by schools in other developing countries facing similar resource constraints.

Pre-print available · Under peer review

Findings submitted to Springer Nature and currently under peer review. A pre-print is publicly available, documenting the cross-country comparison and its implications for scalable STEM education policy.

Project 2 · In progress Cintana International Grant (Microsoft Foundation support)  ·  USD 8,000
2025 – 2026

Exploration of the impact of MicroCode activities (using micro:bit, supported by the Microsoft Foundation) on problem-solving skills, programming skills, and programming interest

What we are doing

This project extends the collaboration beyond unplugged activities into low-cost device-based learning, using the BBC micro:bit and MicroCode — a block-based visual programming environment developed and supported by Microsoft Foundation, designed to make programming accessible to young and inexperienced learners.

The study is being conducted simultaneously in Malaysia and El Salvador, measuring three outcomes: problem-solving skills, programming skills, and programming interest. By combining the micro:bit hardware with MicroCode's accessible visual interface, the project aims to provide a pathway from unplugged activities to device-based programming that remains within the resource reach of under-resourced schools.

Work in progress

Cross-country study underway

Data collection is in progress across Malaysia and El Salvador. Results will assess whether MicroCode + micro:bit activities improve problem-solving ability, programming skills, and student interest in computing.

Accessible hardware, accessible code

The micro:bit + MicroCode combination is being evaluated as a realistic, affordable pathway for schools that have some hardware access but cannot afford specialist software or teacher training.

What comes next

The Cintana–UFG collaboration has established that Malaysia's approach to low-resource STEM education is not unique to Malaysia. The methodology works in El Salvador, and there is no reason to believe it would not work elsewhere. The next step is to document the full evidence base — including the MicroCode findings — and make the programme design available to other institutions and countries facing the same access challenges. If you are interested in replicating this work in your context, we would very much like to hear from you.

Interested in replicating this work in your country or institution?

We are open to extending this collaboration to new countries and contexts. Whether you are a researcher, an educator, or an education organisation working in a low-resource environment, we would like to connect.
